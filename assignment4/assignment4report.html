Part 1) Defining train_nb()

The purpose of the 'train_nb()' function is to approximate the probabilities of each word of the *training* data set being in either the 'positive' or 'negative' classes. The probabilities were returned as logarithmic probabilities, since this lets us leverage the very useful logarithmic property of multipling probabilities by adding their log values instead. This makes for less-demanding computational power, and allows us to multiply very small probability values that would have otherwise appeared as 'zero' values to the python code.

Note that due to time constraints, the sentiment classes (positive reviews or "pos", and negative reviews or "neg") were hardcoded into the python script. (I didn't have time to attempt the optional tasks, unfortunately.)

'train_nb()' takes 'documents' and 'labels' as input parameters, and returns 'class_counts', 'word_counts', 'total_word_counts', 'vocabulary_size'.

Part 2) Defining score_doc_label()

The purpose of the 'score_doc_label()' function is to obtain the *logarithmic* probability of words found in a given 'document' being classified as the requested 'label'. (either 'pos' for positively, or 'neg' for negatively)

Here, the Multinomial Naive Bayes formula is applied as shown below: $$ \hat{P}(w_i | b_j) = \frac{count(w_i,c_j)}{\sum\limits_{w \in V} (w, c_j)}$$ Here, the *posterior* probability, (which is our goal) is found from *prior* term and the *likelihood* term. The prior term represents the probability of a document belonging to a particular class, while the likelihood term represents the probability of seeing a particular word given that class.

However, if we try to find the probability of a word which has not appeared in the entire vocabulary (defined by the training dataset (which is known as V in the formula above), then it errors. So, we apply a *smoothing* filter, as requested by the Assignment 4 instructions. The smoothing filter applied is the 'Laplace smoothing' filter, also known as the 'add 1' smoothing. The modified equation with the smoothing effect is shown below: $$ \hat{P}(w_i | b_j) = \frac{count(w_i,c_j) + 1}{(\sum\limits_{w \in V} count(w, c_j)) + |V|}$$

Some other key points to note:
When trying to classify a new 'document', if a word appears multiple times, it just adds the probability of that word's probability of being classified as a 'positive' or 'negative' sentiment multiple times, which helps reinforce whether that repeated word is classified more as a positive sentiment or a negative sentiment.

As an intuitive example, there is SOME probability, that a word like 'incredible' can be classified as negative, but, by intuition, we would expect that probability to be lower than the word being classified as positive, since in most contexts, it is a happy word.

Part 3) Defining classify_nb()

The function 'classify_nb()' takes the outputs of the 'train_nb()' function and is hardcoded to call the 'score_doc_label()' function for the positive and negative classes. It gets the resulting logarithmic probabilities and compares then against each other. If the probability of the document being positive is higher than being negative, the function will return a classification guess of "pos". If the probability of the document being positive is equal to or lower than being negative, then the function returns a guess of "neg".

The function appears to be functioning correctly. For a test document "['happy', 'and', 'soulful', 'it', 'is', 'a', 'masterpiece']", it was classified as a positive review, while the test document "['kinda', 'boring', 'was', 'a', 'waste', 'of', 'money']" was classified as a negative review.

Part 4) Defining classify_documents()

The function 'classify_documents()' simply scales up the usage of the 'classify_nb()' function to apply to a list of document inputs. Through a *for* loop that iterates through each document in the list, it returns a list of the guessed classifications for each document.

Part 5) Defining accuracy()

The 'accuracy()' function takes the list of guessed labels from the 'classify_documents()' and compares them to the original 'eval_labels' from the given dataset.

The accuracy of this Naive Bayes classification on the 'all_sentiment_shuffled.txt' dataset is 81.54%.

Error Analysis

Out of the 2383 test documents, 440 were misclassified. In the python code, a search loop was implemented to search for examples where it was very heavily misclassified. A heavy misclassification can be defined as having a significant difference between the probability of the document being positive and being negative.

The code compares the logarithm of probabilities. The first check is to see if there is a logarithmic difference of 5 for reviews that are under 50 words. There are no reviews that have a larger difference that is concise, but I also did find an example of a very long review that had a logarithmic probability difference of 90.

The first misclassified review is written below:

"over the last two years , i have found h&r block 's tax cut software to be simple to use to process an otherwise complex tax return . i would recommend it to anyone who wants a reliable tax preparation source without having to pay the accountant 's fees"

This document is a positive review, but was classified as a negative review with the estimated log probability of positivity being -349.20 and the estimated probability of negativity being -341.81.

The sentence above has a mix of positive-sounding words, such as "recommend, reliable", but has more negative-sounding words, such as "cut", "complex", "without", "pay", and "fees". The negativity is addressed to the context of tax return processes, but the Naive Bayes Classifications attributes those words to the sentiment of the entire review, making it think that the reviewer is dissatisfied.

The second misclassified review is written below:

"i bought zonealarm internet security suite for the included zonealarm pro firewall ( it cost much less that way ) . if i did n't already have norton antivirus , i 'd use the antivirus , too"

This document is a positive review, but was classified as a negative review with the estimated log probability of positivity being -255.97 and the estimated log probability of negativity being -247.88.

Like the first misclassification, it has a mix of positive-sounding and negative-sounding words, resulting in mixed sentiment. The wording also has a double negative, stating that it would have used the product if they did not have something already, but the classifier sees those words and amplifies the probability that the review is classified as negative.

The logarithmic probability differences have been very small across the previous misclassfications, but there is one document that has a difference greater than 90. However, it is a 1210 word long document, incorrectly classified as a positive review when the estimated log probability of positivity is -9131.74 and the estimated log probability of negativity being -9222.75.

The review has a lot of contextual description of the relevant historical and political understandings in the book that is being reviewed, and the Naive Bayes filter cannot distinguish between words describing the opinion of the reviewer against the words the author uses to describe certain non-fiction events positively. As such, this document was most likely misclassified due to the relatively large number of positive words.

These examples outline the shortcomings of the Naive Bayes classification, as it treats words as individual features rather than classifying the context the words form in particular orders.
